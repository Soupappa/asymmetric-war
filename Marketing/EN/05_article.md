## Article body

```markdown
## Why the stronger side loses

The short answer: time doesn't work the same way for both sides.

For a great power engaged in an asymmetric conflict, every day of war carries a political, economic, and human cost. The war isn't total for them — it's a limited engagement, fought with a fraction of their resources, for stakes that are often strategically peripheral. But for the other side, it's an existential war. A war of survival.

Kissinger captured it with his characteristic blend of insight and detachment: *"The conventional army loses if it does not win. The guerrilla wins if he does not lose."*

That's the real asymmetry. Not the weapons. Not the budgets. The relationship to time.

## Hand Free — or how winning every battle isn't enough

In Asymmetric Wars, the Coalition doesn't win by destroying its enemy. It wins by destroying *all* real Régime military bases before its own political system collapses.

That fragility is modeled by a metric called **Hand Free**: the product of Economy and Public Support. Both indices are under constant structural pressure. The Economy degrades as war debt rises. Public Support erodes under enemy strikes, civilian casualties, and emergency taxation. Hand Free hitting zero means the Coalition withdraws — regardless of the military situation on the ground.

The Coalition can intercept 95% of missiles. It can destroy dozens of bases. It can maintain uncontested air superiority. And still lose — because the cost of doing so exceeded what its political system could absorb.

That's a history lesson disguised as an idle game mechanic.

## The Régime plays a different game

While the Coalition spends, the Régime disperses.

Build bases, bury them (three depth levels, each requiring more scan cycles to identify), disguise some as civilian installations, build others as decoys to waste precision strikes. Every move the Régime makes is aimed not at winning but at *surviving longer than the adversary's political will*.

The most cynical tool the Régime has is probably the civilian base. Destroyed without scan confirmation, it triggers a massive Coalition public support collapse. With scan confirmation: the Coalition can strike cleanly and even turn opinion in its favor. Without: diplomatic scandal. The game models the moral fog of war — and doesn't adjudicate. It gives you the mechanics and lets you observe the consequences.

## The combat log that talks back

One of the elements I care most about is the satirical log.

In real time, each significant event gets commented on with deliberate deadpan irony. A $20k drone intercepted by a $2M Patriot? The log notes the ratio. A civilian base struck without scan? The log summarizes the diplomatic fallout, quantifies the support losses, and moves on. A drone swarm breaking through after weeks of accumulation? The log announces the milestone with the tone of a press release.

This isn't moralizing. It's observation. The game doesn't say war is bad — it says war has an internal logic, its own economy, and that economy produces objectively absurd situations. The log just names them.

## Observer mode — or "watching the system eat itself"

You can also do nothing.

Observer mode: both AIs play against each other. The Coalition issues war bonds to fund its strikes while its debt climbs. The Régime builds and buries while the Coalition is busy intercepting. The log runs. The macro bars shift. And at some point — not necessarily predictable, not necessarily dramatic — one of the two lines hits its threshold.

It's the most honest experience the game offers: watching two systems apply their own logic until one of them runs out. No intervention. No heroism. No excuse.

## Why an idle game?

Because idle is the genre that best simulates what war actually does: it runs. It consumes. It self-perpetuates. You're not "the general" — you're the decision-maker choosing which doctrine to prioritize, which resource to protect, when to accelerate and when to let the system run.

Automation is a feature. Watching the conflict self-consume is a feature. The post-match report — doctrine used, total strikes, economic collapse mapped — is a feature.

Asymmetric Wars isn't a game about power. It's a game about fragility.
```
